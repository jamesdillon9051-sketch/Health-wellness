The Minimalist Home Gym: What to Buy First (and What to Skip)

Four purchases cover about ninety per cent of what a home trainee needs. Here they are in buying order, with an honest account of what each unlocks and what to ignore entirely.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

Home gym advice tends to describe a room. This describes four purchases, because for the overwhelming majority of people training at home around a job, four is where the useful returns stop.

The order matters more than the individual choices, and the first instruction is to buy nothing at all.

The principle: buy for gaps, not for completeness

The instinct when equipping a home gym is to assemble a set — something for legs, something for chest, something for back. That instinct produces redundancy, because bodyweight training already covers most of it.

The better question is: what can I not currently train? Bodyweight training has exactly one structural gap, plus a few things that become limitations later. Buy for those and nothing else.

Four of the five movement patterns — squat, hinge, push, brace — work with a floor and some furniture. The fifth, pull, requires resistance to pull against. That asymmetry drives the entire buying order below. The patterns are set out in the complete guide to bodyweight strength training at home.

Start by buying nothing

Train for two months with no equipment before spending anything. This is the most useful advice on this page and the least followed.

Equipment does not create consistency. The failure mode in home training is almost always that the routine stopped, and buying things is an effective way of feeling like you have started without having started. A shelf of unused equipment is the standard outcome of getting this backwards.

In those two months, cover pulling temporarily with a towel around a door handle or inverted rows under a sturdy table — both work, and both are described in rows at home. Load exercises with a rucksack full of books if you need to, per DIY home gym equipment.

After two months you will know which gap you actually hit, which is nearly always pulling, rather than which one you imagined you would.

1. Something to pull against

The only purchase that changes what is trainable rather than what is comfortable. Everything else on this list improves training you can already do.

Resistance bands

The default recommendation, because they work in every home regardless of door frames, floor type, ceiling height or tenancy terms. They also load overhead pressing, lateral raises, face pulls and direct arm work — none of which bodyweight reaches well.

Buy a set with at least three strengths plus a door anchor. Long loop bands are more versatile and can assist pull-ups; tube bands with handles are more comfortable for pressing. The comparison is in loop bands vs tube bands, the full detail in the resistance bands guide, and a complete session in the resistance band full-body workout.

Doorway pull-up bar

Buy this instead or as well, if a suitable frame exists. A bar unlocks hanging, rows at any angle and eventually pull-ups — the most satisfying strength milestone available at home, with the route in how to do your first pull-up at home.

It is also the one item on this list with a genuine safety dimension. Buy a leverage-mount bar rather than a telescopic pressure-fit one, check the rated capacity against your bodyweight with a real margin, and check your door frames before budgeting for it — plenty of homes have none that are suitable. All of this is in the doorway pull-up bar guide.

Rings or a suspension trainer

The third route, and a good one if you have somewhere to hang it. Rings are more capable and cheaper but need a structural anchor; a suspension trainer solves the anchoring problem with a door strap and costs more. The comparison is in gymnastic rings at home and suspension trainers at home.

Whichever you choose, buy one of these three. They solve the same problem, and owning all three is the commonest way people overspend.

2. A mat, if your floor demands it

Not universal. On carpet with underlay you can skip this entirely.

On a hard floor it matters, because uncomfortable exercises get quietly dropped from sessions — and the ones that get dropped are the floor-based core and glute work that home routines are already short of. Above a neighbour it matters for a different reason.

Buy density over thickness. If you can compress it flat between finger and thumb, your bodyweight will do the same. Around 12 to 15 millimetres of dense EVA foam is the sensible target, in interlocking tiles so you can cover the area you actually use — roughly two metres by one, including under your hands, which is the coverage mistake almost everyone makes.

The comparison of types is in yoga mat vs exercise mat vs puzzle tiles, and the noise analysis in exercise mats for noise reduction.

3. Furniture sliders

The cheapest item here by a wide margin and genuinely useful, which is an unusual combination.

They unlock sliding hamstring curls, which train knee flexion — a function home leg training otherwise misses almost entirely, because squats, lunges and split squats are all knee-dominant and the hamstrings only work as hip extensors. That is a real gap, and sliders are the cheapest way to close it.

Felt-bottomed for hard floors, hard plastic for carpet. Furniture-moving packs usually include both and cost less than fitness-branded versions. A folded towel on a hard floor works almost as well, so test the idea free first. The exercises are in furniture sliders for core and leg training.

4. Added load

The last purchase, and later than most people expect — typically one to two years of consistent training.

Bodyweight progression works by making movements harder, and it eventually runs into a wall where the next progression is limited by skill or joint tolerance rather than by the muscle you meant to train. At that point adding load to a movement you have already mastered is cleaner than learning a harder one. The evidence on where that wall sits is in can you build muscle with bodyweight only.

A loaded rucksack does this for nothing and is the right first move — see DIY home gym equipment.

A weighted vest is more comfortable above about fifteen kilograms and worth buying at that point — see weighted vest training.

One adjustable dumbbell is the more versatile option, because used one side at a time it covers overhead pressing, rows, split squats, single-leg hinges and carries. The session is in the one dumbbell workout, the buying criteria in the adjustable dumbbells buying guide, and the kettlebell alternative in kettlebell vs dumbbell for a small home setup.

Only one purchase changes what you can train. Everything else changes how comfortably you train what you already could.

Why minimal beats comprehensive

The argument for a small equipment list is usually made on cost and space. Those are real, and there is a third reason that matters more.

More equipment produces worse programmes. Given ten pieces of equipment, most people build sessions that use all ten, which means one or two sets of each exercise. Given three, they build sessions of three or four movements done for three or four hard sets. The second is a considerably better training stimulus, because weekly volume per movement pattern is what drives adaptation3 and spreading effort thinly across many exercises accumulates very little of it anywhere.

More equipment produces more decisions. Every session becomes a choice about what to use, and choices are friction. A setup with four items has essentially one sensible session in it, which sounds limiting and is in fact the point — you turn up and do the thing rather than deciding what the thing is.

More equipment means more that has to be set up. The steps between deciding to train and starting are where sessions get lost. A rack that needs assembling costs more sessions than it enables.

This is why the list above stops at four rather than continuing to a well-equipped garage. It is not a budget compromise; past a certain point, additional equipment makes home training worse rather than better.

Five buying mistakes

1. Buying before establishing the habit. The single commonest one. Two months of training with nothing tells you what you need; two months of research tells you what is available, which is a different and less useful thing.

2. Buying three solutions to one problem. Bands, a bar, rings and a suspension trainer all address pulling. Owning several is not a more complete gym; it is the same gap solved repeatedly.

3. Buying for the training you might do. Equipment for a programme you are not currently running rarely gets used. Buy for the sessions in your calendar this month.

4. Buying capacity you will not reach. A dumbbell adjustable to forty kilograms costs more, is bulkier and has a more failure-prone mechanism than one adjustable to twenty-four, and most home trainees never approach the ceiling of either.

5. Confusing equipment with progress. The most expensive mistake and the least visible. Buying something produces a genuine feeling of momentum that lasts about a week and is entirely unrelated to whether you trained. If you notice yourself researching purchases more than training, that is the signal — and the fix is in how to actually stick to a home workout habit.

Buying second-hand

Worth doing for some categories and not others.

Good used: dumbbells, kettlebells and plates. Cast iron does not wear out, a scratched dumbbell performs identically to a new one, and they are heavily discounted because shipping weight makes them expensive to sell online. This is the best used-equipment category by a distance.

Acceptable used: pull-up bars, benches and rings, provided you can inspect them. Check for deformation, cracked welds, loose bolts and compressed pads, and reject anything with visible bending.

Avoid used: resistance bands and anything else made of latex. They degrade with age, ultraviolet light and use, and you cannot assess how a set has been stored. A band that fails under tension recoils toward your face, and new ones are cheap enough that this is not a saving worth making.

What to skip

A bench, until you own dumbbells and chest work is a priority. Without dumbbells it unlocks almost nothing a dining chair does not, and it is physically the largest thing you might buy — see do you need a bench for home workouts.

Duplicate solutions to pulling. Bands, a bar, rings and a suspension trainer all address the same gap. Buy one.

Push-up handles. The range increase is real and small, and two books do the same thing.

An ab wheel, initially. Excellent exercise, far too hard for most beginners, and sliders do nearly the same movement more versatilely — see the ab wheel progression.

Very light dumbbells. Too light for anything but rehabilitation, and a band covers that better.

A skipping rope, if you live above someone. Excellent equipment, unusable in a flat — the alternatives are in jump rope alternatives.

Anything promising passive results. Vibrating plates, EMS belts and similar. The evidence for meaningful strength or body composition change is weak.

An app subscription, at least initially — see home workout apps: what is worth paying for.

Equipment and small spaces

In a flat, storage is a selection criterion rather than an afterthought. Equipment that must be retrieved from another room is equipment you have stopped owning, because every step between deciding to train and starting is a place the decision reverses.

Fortunately the four items above store well: bands on a hook, tiles stacked flat, sliders in a drawer, a bar left in the frame or behind a door. The whole list occupies less than a suitcase. The detail is in how to store home gym equipment in a tiny flat and setting up a home gym corner.

Three budgets

Nothing. A towel on a door handle for rows, a sturdy table for inverted rows, a rucksack of books for load, stairs for conditioning and platform heights. This is a complete programme, and it is what the first two months should look like regardless of budget.

Under about $50. Resistance bands and furniture sliders. Closes the pulling gap and the hamstring gap, which are the two genuine holes. See the best cheap home gym equipment under $50.

Under about $100. Add a doorway pull-up bar and foam tiles. At that point every movement pattern is covered at every difficulty a home trainee needs for years — the full list is in the home gym setup under $100.

Beyond that, spending buys convenience and comfort rather than capability, until you reach the point where added load becomes the limiting factor.

Where to start

Train for two months with nothing. Use the 4-week beginner plan if you are new, or the three-day split if you are not.

Then buy one solution to pulling. Then stop, and train for another two months before buying anything else.

Adult activity guidance asks for muscle-strengthening activity involving all major muscle groups on two or more days a week, alongside regular aerobic activity.1 None of it requires equipment. And light loads taken close to failure produce muscle growth comparable to heavy loads,2 so the modest resistance available at home is far less of a limitation than the equipment industry implies. Weekly volume and effort are what drive the result,3 and neither is for sale.

Frequently asked questions

What should I buy first for a home gym?

Something to pull against — resistance bands, a doorway pull-up bar, rings or a suspension trainer. Pulling is the only movement pattern bodyweight training cannot supply, so it is the only purchase that changes what is trainable rather than how comfortable training is.

Do I need equipment to work out at home?

Only for pulling, and even that can be covered temporarily with a towel around a door handle or rows under a sturdy table. Adult activity guidance requires no equipment, and light loads taken close to failure produce comparable muscle growth to heavy ones.

How much should I spend on a home gym?

Under about $100 covers every movement pattern at every difficulty a home trainee needs for years. Beyond that, spending buys convenience rather than capability until added load becomes the limiting factor, usually after one to two years.

What home gym equipment is not worth buying?

A bench until you own dumbbells, duplicate solutions to pulling, push-up handles, an ab wheel before you can hold a solid plank, very light dumbbells, and anything promising passive results such as vibrating plates or EMS belts.

When should I buy weights for home training?

When bodyweight variations have genuinely run out, typically after one to two years of consistent training. A loaded rucksack does the job for nothing and is the right first move; one adjustable dumbbell is the more versatile purchase after that.

Should I buy equipment before starting to train at home?

No. Train with nothing for two months first. Equipment does not create consistency, and buying things is an effective way of feeling like you have started without having started.

Is a pull-up bar or resistance bands better?

Bands work in every home regardless of door frames, floor type or tenancy terms, and additionally load overhead pressing. A bar unlocks hanging and pull-ups. Buy one to start with — they solve the same gap, and owning both is a later refinement rather than a necessity.
