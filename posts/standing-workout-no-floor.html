Standing Workouts: No Getting Up and Down Off the Floor

Getting down to the floor and back up again is the barrier for more people than any exercise is. Here is a full session that never requires it.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

What's in this guide

  3. The session
  4. Standing core work
  5. What you lose, and what replaces it

A surprising amount of home training assumes you can get down to the floor and back up again repeatedly without difficulty. For plenty of people that assumption is wrong, and the result is a session that looks fine on paper and never gets done.

This is a complete strength session performed entirely at standing height.

Who this is for

Several situations, some obvious and some less so:

  • Knee, hip or back problems that make kneeling or getting up from the floor painful.
  • Reduced mobility or deconditioning, where floor transfers are genuinely effortful.
  • Dirty or unpleasant floors — a real barrier, and one people are oddly embarrassed to name.
  • Very small spaces with no room to lie down, per how much floor space you need.
  • Office or work-clothes training, covered in the office workout.
  • Late-night training where floor contact is the noise source.
If pain is the reason

If you are avoiding floor exercises because of knee, hip or back pain, that is worth investigating rather than working around indefinitely. A physiotherapist can usually tell you whether the movement should be avoided or gradually rebuilt. This session is a good way to keep training in the meantime, not a substitute for finding out what is going on.

The exercises

Squat pattern

Sit-to-stand. From a chair, standing without using your hands. Scalable by chair height — higher is easier. The single most useful exercise for anyone whose floor transfers are difficult, because it trains exactly the capacity that makes them difficult.

Split squat holding support. One hand on a wall or worktop. Eight to twelve per leg, and the main strength exercise here — see Bulgarian split squats at home for the elevated version once this is easy.

Push pattern

Wall or counter push-up. Full range, precisely scalable by moving your feet. The whole incline ladder is available standing, per the push-up progressions.

Pull pattern

Door frame row or band row. Grip the frame of an open door and lean back, or use a band anchored at the hinge. Both are done standing. Options in rows at home.

Hinge pattern

Standing hip hinge. Push the hips back with a flat back, lower the torso to about forty-five degrees, return by driving the hips forward. Add a light weight in the hands to load it.

Single-leg Romanian deadlift. The harder version, holding a wall for balance. Trains the hinge and pelvic stability simultaneously.

Calves

Standing calf raise. Off a step edge for extra range if you can manage the step safely.

The session

Warm up with two minutes of marching and arm swings. Rest sixty to ninety seconds between sets. About twenty-five minutes.

  • Split squat holding support — 3 × 8–12 each leg
  • Counter push-up — 3 × 10–15
  • Door frame or band row — 3 × 10–15
  • Standing hip hinge — 3 × 12–15
  • Sit-to-stand — 2 × 10–15
  • Calf raise — 3 × 15–25
  • Standing anti-rotation hold — 3 × 20 seconds each side

That covers all five movement patterns without a single floor exercise. Adult guidance asks for strengthening work across all major muscle groups on two or more days a week,1 and this clears it.

Standing core work

The part people assume is impossible, and it is not — because the trunk's real job is resisting movement rather than producing it, which can be done upright. The four functions are explained in core training without sit-ups.

Anti-rotation hold. Hold a resistance band anchored at chest height, standing side-on, arms extended in front. Resist the pull for twenty to thirty seconds. The best standing core exercise available.

Suitcase carry. Carry something heavy in one hand and walk, keeping the torso vertical rather than leaning. Unglamorous, genuinely effective, and entirely standing.

Standing march with a pause. Lift one knee to hip height and hold for three seconds without letting the pelvis drop. Trains pelvic stability.

Wall-supported hollow hold. Standing with your back flat against a wall, brace hard and press the lower back into the wall for twenty seconds.

What you lose, and what replaces it

Three things are genuinely harder to train standing.

Direct abdominal loading. Dead bugs and hollow holds have no exact standing equivalent. Anti-rotation holds and carries cover the same functions differently, which is most of the way there.

The hardest pressing variations. Decline and deficit push-ups need floor contact. The counter and wall ladder tops out lower, though it takes most people a long time to reach that ceiling.

Glute bridges. Genuinely floor-based. Single-leg Romanian deadlifts and split squats cover the same muscles by a different route — see glute exercises at home.

None of these gaps prevent a productive programme. They narrow the exercise selection rather than the results.

Progressing standing exercises

The same levers apply as anywhere else: get closer to failure, slow the tempo, increase the range, change leverage, reduce limbs. With a fixed load, effort is the main intensity variable, and evidence suggests light loads taken close to failure produce comparable adaptation to heavy ones.2

Specifically: lower your hands on the push-up ladder, lower the chair for sit-to-stands, move to single-leg versions of the hinge, and add a rucksack with books for loaded standing work — see DIY home gym equipment. The general ladders are in bodyweight exercise progressions.

Working toward floor exercises

If the reason you are avoiding the floor is strength or mobility rather than a permanent condition, this session is also a route back.

Sit-to-stands are the key exercise, because getting up from the floor is essentially the same movement pattern from a lower start. Lower the chair over months — dining chair, then a low stool, then a stack of books — and floor transfers become progressively easier.

Add half-kneeling positions once that feels manageable: kneeling on one knee and standing up, holding support. Then kneeling on both. This is a genuine progression and worth treating as one rather than assuming the floor will simply become available one day.

Frequently asked questions

Can you get a full workout standing up?

Yes. Split squats, counter push-ups, door frame or band rows, standing hip hinges, sit-to-stands, calf raises and standing anti-rotation holds cover all five movement patterns without a single floor exercise.

What standing exercises work the core?

Anti-rotation holds against a resistance band, suitcase carries with a heavy object in one hand, standing marches with a three-second pause, and wall-supported bracing. The trunk mostly works to resist movement, which can be trained upright.

Why is getting up from the floor so hard?

It requires a large range of hip and knee motion under load, plus the strength to stand from a very low position. Sit-to-stands from a progressively lower chair train exactly that capacity and are the best route back to comfortable floor transfers.

Are standing workouts as effective as floor workouts?

For most purposes, close to it. The gaps are direct abdominal loading, the hardest push-up variations, and glute bridges — all of which have standing alternatives that cover the same functions by a different route.

Can I build muscle with standing exercises only?

Yes. The same progression levers apply: getting closer to failure, slowing the tempo, increasing range, changing leverage and moving to single-leg variations. Light loads taken close to failure produce comparable adaptation to heavy ones.
