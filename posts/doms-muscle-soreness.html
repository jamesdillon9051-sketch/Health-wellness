DOMS: Why You're Sore and What Actually Helps

Soreness tracks novelty far more than it tracks progress. Understanding that changes both how you interpret it and what you bother doing about it.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

Delayed onset muscle soreness is the stiffness and tenderness that arrives twelve to twenty-four hours after unfamiliar exercise, peaks around forty-eight hours, and fades over the following few days.

It is one of the most misinterpreted signals in training, and the misinterpretation has consequences: people chase it, judge sessions by it, and abandon programmes when it stops appearing.

What DOMS actually is

The short answer is that the mechanism is not fully settled, and anyone telling you it is definitely one thing is overstating the evidence.

What is reasonably established: it involves microscopic damage to muscle fibres and connective tissue, followed by an inflammatory response, and it is associated particularly with eccentric contractions — the lowering phase, where the muscle lengthens under load.

What is not established: the older explanation that lactic acid causes it. Lactate clears within about an hour of exercise, long before soreness peaks. That explanation is still widely repeated and is wrong.

Why it happens

The strongest predictor is novelty. A movement your body has not done recently produces soreness; the same movement done regularly produces very little, even at higher intensities.

This is why the sliding hamstring curl in the sliders guide reliably produces substantial soreness on first exposure — not because it is uniquely damaging, but because the knee-flexion function is almost never trained in home routines.

It is also why the same programme produces dramatic soreness in week one and almost none by week six. The training has not become less effective; your body has adapted to the specific movements. This is sometimes called the repeated bout effect, and it happens quickly — often after a single session.

Soreness is not a scorecard

The practical consequence, and the most useful thing on this page.

If soreness tracks novelty, then it is a poor measure of whether a session was productive. A well-designed programme repeats the same movements for six to eight weeks and progresses their difficulty — which means soreness should decline while results improve. Judging by soreness would tell you the opposite.

Two specific errors follow from getting this wrong. Changing your programme constantly to keep feeling sore, which destroys your ability to measure progress. And concluding a session was wasted because you woke up fine, when weekly volume and proximity to failure are what actually drive adaptation.1

Week one hurts and week six does not. Week six is when you are actually getting stronger.

What the evidence supports

A systematic review with meta-analysis by Dupuy and colleagues examined post-exercise recovery techniques for their effects on muscle damage markers, soreness, fatigue and inflammation.2 Two broad conclusions are worth taking from that literature.

Massage has reasonable support for reducing perceived soreness. Effects are modest rather than dramatic.

Cold and heat therapy have some support. A meta-analysis of thirty-two randomised controlled trials found heat and cold therapy reduced pain in people with DOMS.3 Again, modest effects — useful for comfort rather than transformative.

Light movement helps in the moment. Gentle activity temporarily reduces the sensation, which is why sore legs feel better once you have been walking for five minutes. This does not appear to accelerate recovery, but it makes the day more pleasant.

Time works reliably. Unhelpful as advice, and true. DOMS resolves in three to five days regardless.

What does not work

Stretching. A Cochrane review found that stretching before or after exercise does not produce clinically important reductions in muscle soreness.4 This is one of the more firmly established negative findings in the area, and it contradicts extremely widespread advice. Stretching is a reasonable way to work on range of motion; it is not soreness insurance — see cool-down stretches.

Foam rolling, mostly. There is some evidence for short-term effects on perceived soreness and range of motion, but the effects are small and the mechanistic claims made for it are weak — the detail is in foam rolling: what the evidence says.

Extra protein specifically for soreness. Adequate protein supports muscle adaptation,5 which is a good reason to get enough — see how much protein you need. Taking more than adequate to reduce soreness is not supported.

Should you train when sore?

Generally yes, with two qualifications.

Mild to moderate soreness is not a reason to skip a session. Performance may be slightly reduced, and training through it does not appear to cause harm. Training a different muscle group is straightforward; training the same one is usually fine at a reduced intensity.

Reduce the load if soreness is severe enough to alter your technique. Compensating for sore muscles by changing how you move is how people accumulate niggles elsewhere.

Take soreness that has not cleared by the next session of that type as a signal. Not of injury, but that your volume has outrun your recovery. That is one of the criteria for a lighter week described in deload weeks for home training.

When soreness is worth worrying about

Seek medical advice if

Soreness is accompanied by significant swelling, by dark or cola-coloured urine, or by severe pain out of proportion to the exercise you did. That combination can indicate rhabdomyolysis, a rare but serious condition where damaged muscle releases proteins into the bloodstream. It is uncommon and it is a medical emergency, most often following an unusually intense unaccustomed session.

Also seek advice for sharp or localised pain rather than diffuse muscular ache, pain in a joint rather than a muscle, or any pain that worsens rather than improves after seventy-two hours.

Ordinary DOMS is diffuse, affects the whole muscle, feels worse on movement and better on light activity, and improves steadily from around day three.

Reducing it

Prevention works considerably better than treatment.

Introduce new exercises gradually. Two sets rather than four the first time you do a movement. Most severe DOMS comes from a first exposure done at full volume.

Do not chase novelty. A programme that repeats its movements produces adaptation and very little soreness — the argument for keeping a session for six to eight weeks, per bodyweight exercise progressions.

Progress gradually. Large jumps in volume or difficulty are what produce the worst episodes.

Sleep. Its effects on recovery and performance are reasonably documented,6 and it is the recovery intervention with the best evidence behind it — see sleep and strength.

Frequently asked questions

What causes DOMS?

The mechanism is not fully settled. It involves microscopic damage to muscle fibres and connective tissue followed by an inflammatory response, and it is strongly associated with eccentric contractions. It is not caused by lactic acid, which clears within about an hour of exercise.

Does soreness mean a workout was effective?

No. Soreness tracks novelty far more than it tracks progress. The same programme produces dramatic soreness in week one and almost none by week six, while the results improve over that period.

What actually helps with muscle soreness?

Massage has reasonable support, and a meta-analysis of thirty-two trials found heat and cold therapy reduce DOMS pain. Effects are modest rather than dramatic. Light movement makes the day more comfortable without accelerating recovery.

Does stretching help muscle soreness?

No. A Cochrane review found stretching before or after exercise does not produce clinically important reductions in muscle soreness. It is a reasonable way to work on range of motion, but it is not soreness prevention.

Should I work out when sore?

Generally yes. Mild to moderate soreness is not a reason to skip a session, though performance may be slightly reduced. Reduce the load if soreness is severe enough to change your technique, and treat soreness that has not cleared by the next session as a sign your volume has outrun recovery.

When is muscle soreness a medical concern?

Seek medical advice if soreness comes with significant swelling, dark or cola-coloured urine, or severe pain out of proportion to the exercise. That combination can indicate rhabdomyolysis, which is rare but a medical emergency. Also seek advice for sharp or joint pain, or pain worsening after seventy-two hours.
