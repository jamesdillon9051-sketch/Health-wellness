A 4-Week Home Workout Plan for Complete Beginners

Twelve sessions over four weeks, no equipment, every movement scaled to where you actually are. Built on the assumption that you will miss at least one session, because everyone does.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

Most beginner plans fail for the same reason: they are written for the person you intend to be rather than the person starting on Monday. They assume you will complete every session, that nothing will overrun, and that motivation in week one predicts behaviour in week four. None of those hold.

This plan is built the other way round. It has twelve sessions across four weeks, deliberately fewer than you could probably manage, so that a missed evening does not put you behind. Every exercise has three difficulty levels so nothing is out of reach or pointlessly easy. And the progression is slow enough that week four is achievable even if week two went badly.

Before you start

If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, joint problems, are pregnant or recently postpartum, or have been inactive for a long period, speak to your doctor before beginning. Stop and seek advice if you experience chest pain or tightness, dizziness, an irregular heartbeat, or sharp joint pain. Muscular effort and mild breathlessness are expected; those symptoms are not.

Who this plan is for

People who have not trained consistently before, or who have not trained in a long time. You do not need any equipment, any particular fitness level, or more than about half an hour three times a week.

You should be able to get down to the floor and back up again, and stand from a chair without using your hands. If either is difficult right now, the standing-only variations in standing workouts are a better starting point, and this plan will be there afterwards.

It is deliberately not a fat-loss plan. Training contributes far less to weight change than most people expect, and the honest version of that argument is in the realistic 8-week weight-loss plan. What this plan builds is strength and, more importantly, a routine.

The four rules this plan runs on

1. Three sessions a week, never two in a row. Monday, Wednesday, Friday is the default. Any spacing works provided there is a day between sessions — muscle adapts during recovery, not during the work.

2. Stop every set with reps left in reserve. For the first three weeks, stop when you could still do three or four more. This feels far too easy and it is deliberate: soreness in week one is the single most common reason people quit in week two.

3. Never miss two sessions in a row. One missed session is irrelevant. Two consecutive misses is where routines start unravelling, and the third becomes much easier to miss than the second.

4. When in doubt, go easier. An easier session that happens beats a harder one that leaves you unable to face the next. Nothing in the first four weeks is improved by pushing.

The six movements and how to scale them

The plan uses six exercises and never changes them. This is intentional — you cannot tell whether you are improving if the test keeps changing. Each has three levels; start at whichever gives you the target repetitions with three or four still in reserve.

The six movements with three difficulty levels each
MovementLevel 1Level 2Level 3
SquatSit-to-stand from a chairBodyweight squat to a chairFull bodyweight squat
PushWall push-upCounter or table push-upFloor push-up
HingeGlute bridge, both feetGlute bridge, pause at topSingle-leg glute bridge
LungeSplit stance, hold supportReverse lunge, hold supportReverse lunge, no support
PullTowel row on door handleRow under a tableRow with feet forward
CoreDead bug, feet downDead bug, feet upDead bug, full extension

The pull is the one that needs something. A towel looped around a sturdy door handle works and costs nothing; a table you trust is better. This movement matters more than it looks — it is the one home routines skip, and its absence shows within months. The options are set out in rows at home.

The warm-up, every session

Two minutes, unchanged for four weeks: thirty seconds marching on the spot, ten slow squats to a comfortable depth, ten arm circles each direction, five wall push-ups. Do not skip it and do not extend it. A longer version is in the five-minute warm-up if you wake up stiff.

Week 1: learning the movements

Two sets of each movement. Rest 60–90 seconds between sets. Roughly 20 minutes.

  • Squat — 2 × 10
  • Push — 2 × 8
  • Hinge — 2 × 12
  • Lunge — 2 × 6 each leg
  • Pull — 2 × 8
  • Core — 2 × 6 each side

The entire goal this week is to finish all three sessions feeling like you could have done more. If you are sore enough on Tuesday to dread Wednesday, you went too hard — drop a level and continue. Some soreness after the first session is normal and is not a measure of how well it went; the evidence on that is covered in DOMS: why you're sore and what actually helps.

Write down which level you used and how the last set felt. This takes ten seconds and it is the only record that will tell you anything in week four.

Week 2: adding a set

Three sets of the first three movements, two of the rest. Roughly 25 minutes.

  • Squat — 3 × 10
  • Push — 3 × 8
  • Hinge — 3 × 12
  • Lunge — 2 × 8 each leg
  • Pull — 2 × 10
  • Core — 2 × 8 each side

Still stopping with three or four repetitions in reserve. Weekly volume is what drives adaptation,1 and adding a set is the cleanest way to increase it without making any individual set harder.

If any movement felt genuinely easy in week one — you finished the set barely noticing it — move up a level now rather than waiting.

Week 3: getting closer to hard

Three sets of everything. Roughly 28 minutes.

  • Squat — 3 × 12
  • Push — 3 × 10
  • Hinge — 3 × 12, one-second pause at the top
  • Lunge — 3 × 8 each leg
  • Pull — 3 × 10
  • Core — 3 × 8 each side

This week, take the final set only of the squat and the push closer to failure — one or two repetitions in reserve rather than three or four. Everything else stays comfortable.

This is the first genuinely hard training you will have done, and it matters. With no external load, proximity to failure is the main intensity lever available: evidence comparing light and heavy loads found comparable muscle growth when sets were taken close to failure.2 You do not need weight; you need effort, applied sparingly and deliberately.

Week 4: the first real test

Three sets, final set of every movement close to failure. Roughly 30 minutes.

  • Squat — 3 × 12–15
  • Push — 3 × 10–12
  • Hinge — 3 × 15 with pause
  • Lunge — 3 × 10 each leg
  • Pull — 3 × 12
  • Core — 3 × 10 each side

On the final session of week four, do one extra set of the squat and the push to genuine failure and write down the number. That is your baseline. Compare it with what you wrote in week one, and it is likely to be considerably higher — early gains in untrained people come substantially from the nervous system learning the movement, which is why progress in the first month often outpaces anything visible in the mirror.

The plan succeeds if you start week five. Everything else — the reps, the levels, the soreness — is secondary to that one outcome.

One form cue that matters for each movement

Beginners are usually given too many technique instructions at once, which produces someone thinking about six things and doing none of them. Here is the single cue that fixes the most common error in each movement. Learn one at a time.

Squat — push the floor apart with your feet. This stops the knees collapsing inward without you having to think about your knees at all, which is useful because consciously forcing the knees out usually creates a different problem. Depth and heel position are covered properly in bodyweight squat form.

Push — keep your elbows at about forty-five degrees. Not flared straight out to the sides, which is hard on the shoulders, and not clamped to your ribs, which is unnecessarily difficult. Somewhere in between, with the body in one line from ear to ankle. More detail in how to do a perfect push-up.

Hinge — squeeze rather than arch. At the top of a glute bridge the work should be felt in the glutes, not the lower back. If your back is doing the lifting, you have gone too high. Stop where the squeeze is strongest.

Lunge — step back, not forward. Stepping back puts noticeably less shear through the front knee and is easier to control. Holding a wall or doorframe for balance is a scaling option, not a failure.

Pull — lead with the elbows. Think about driving the elbows back past your ribs rather than pulling with your hands. This recruits the back rather than turning the movement into an arm exercise.

Core — keep the lower back flat on the floor. The dead bug is a test of whether you can stop your back arching, not of how far you can extend. Shorten the range as soon as the back lifts; that shortened range is the correct range for you today.

If a cue does not click, ignore it and do the movement at an easier level. Technique improves substantially just from repetition at a difficulty you can control, which is most of the argument for starting easier than you think you need to.

When you miss a session

You will, and the plan is built for it. Three rules:

Missed one session: shift it. Do it the next day and push the rest of the week along. Do not skip forward to stay on the calendar.

Missed a whole week: repeat the last week you completed rather than continuing where the plan says. Losing seven days costs very little; trying to jump back in two weeks ahead of where your body is costs a great deal more.

Missed two weeks or more: restart at week one, at the level you were using. This is not a setback so much as a slightly shorter version of the same four weeks. There is a fuller approach in how to restart after missing two weeks.

What matters far more than any of this is not missing two consecutive planned sessions. If Monday goes, protect Wednesday even if it means doing ten minutes of the first three movements and nothing else. Having that reduced version decided in advance is one of the more useful things you can do — the reasoning is in how to actually stick to a home workout habit.

What to do after week four

Three sensible options, depending on how it went.

If you completed 10 or more of the 12 sessions: move to the three-day home workout split. It uses the same movement patterns at a higher difficulty and is designed to run for eight weeks.

If you completed 7 to 9: run this plan again from week two, at whatever levels you finished on. The habit is forming but is not solid yet, and another month at this intensity is a better investment than a harder programme you will complete less often.

If you completed fewer than 7: the problem is not the training, and a different plan will not fix it. Reduce to two sessions a week — which still meets the adult recommendation for muscle-strengthening activity3 — and work on the scheduling side. The weekly schedule guide covers anchoring sessions to events rather than intentions.

Whichever applies, do not increase to five or six days a week at this point. The most common failure after a successful first month is treating early enthusiasm as evidence of sustainable capacity. How many days is genuinely right is covered in how many days a week should you work out.

What to expect, honestly

Weeks 1–2: some soreness, particularly after the first two sessions. Movements feel awkward. Nothing visible changes, and nothing should.

Week 3: the movements start feeling coordinated rather than effortful in a clumsy way. Soreness largely stops. This is real adaptation, mostly neurological.

Week 4: measurably more repetitions than week one, often substantially more. Stairs and carrying things feel different. Possibly better sleep.

What you should not expect: visible muscle change, meaningful weight change, or a transformed body. Four weeks is not long enough for any of those, and a plan promising them is not being straight with you. Realistic timelines are in how long until you see results from home workouts.

What four weeks is long enough for is establishing that you are someone who trains three times a week. That is the outcome worth aiming at, and it is the one that determines everything that happens in month six.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 4-week workout plan long enough to see results?

Long enough to see clear strength improvement, yes — most beginners add substantial repetitions over four weeks, largely through neurological adaptation. Not long enough for visible muscle change or meaningful weight change, and any plan promising those in four weeks is overselling.

Do I need any equipment for this plan?

Only for the pulling movement, which needs something to pull against — a towel around a sturdy door handle, or a table you trust. Everything else uses bodyweight, a wall, a chair and the floor.

How many days a week should a beginner work out?

Three, with at least one rest day between sessions. Two is a legitimate floor and meets the adult recommendation for muscle-strengthening activity. Five or six days is a common early mistake that leads to abandoning the routine within two months.

Should I be sore after every workout?

No. Some soreness in the first week or two is normal, but persistent soreness usually means the intensity is too high for where you are. Soreness tracks novelty far more than it tracks progress, and it is a poor measure of whether a session was useful.

What if I miss a week of the plan?

Repeat the last week you completed rather than continuing where the calendar says. If you miss two weeks or more, restart at week one using the levels you had reached. Neither is a setback worth worrying about.

What should I do after finishing the 4 weeks?

If you completed ten or more of the twelve sessions, move to a three-day split at higher difficulty. If you completed seven to nine, run the plan again from week two. If you completed fewer than seven, reduce to two sessions a week and work on scheduling rather than programming.

Can I do this plan if I am very unfit?

Yes. Every movement has three levels, and level one uses a wall, a chair and support for balance. If getting down to the floor and back up is currently difficult, start with a standing-only routine and come to this plan afterwards.
