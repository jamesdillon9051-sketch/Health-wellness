EMOM Workouts at Home: How to Program Them

Every Minute On the Minute is the most underrated format for home training, because it solves the one problem bodyweight work has: knowing when to stop.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

What's in this guide

  1. How EMOM works
  2. Why it suits bodyweight training
  3. Choosing the rep counts
  4. Three EMOM sessions
  5. How to progress an EMOM
  6. Where people go wrong

EMOM stands for Every Minute On the Minute. You start a set of an exercise at the top of each minute, complete the prescribed repetitions, and rest for whatever remains of that minute. When the next minute starts, you go again.

If the set takes forty seconds, you rest twenty. If you slow down and it takes fifty, you rest ten. The format is self-regulating in a way that makes it unusually well suited to training with no equipment.

How EMOM works

A twelve-minute EMOM alternating two exercises means six sets of each, with rest determined by how fast you work. That is the whole structure.

What makes it different from a circuit is that the work-to-rest ratio is not fixed by you in advance — it emerges from your performance. As you fatigue, sets take longer, so rest shrinks automatically, so the session gets progressively harder without you deciding anything. It builds in a natural intensity curve.

It also gives you a clean, honest performance metric. If the same twelve-minute EMOM leaves you with twenty-five seconds of rest per minute this week and fifteen seconds last week, you have improved, and you have a number rather than an impression.

Why it suits bodyweight training

Three reasons, and the first is the important one.

It solves the stopping problem. The hardest thing about training with no equipment is knowing when a set should end. With a barbell, the weight decides. With push-ups, you either count to an arbitrary number or go to failure — and going to failure on every set costs more recovery than it returns.1 An EMOM sets the number externally and holds it constant, which removes the decision entirely.

It enforces rest. Home training drifts toward continuous work because rest feels unproductive. EMOM makes rest structural: if you finish early, you rest, because starting the next set early breaks the format.

It accumulates real volume in short sessions. A fifteen-minute EMOM with eight repetitions per minute is 120 repetitions. Weekly volume drives growth,2 and this format packs a lot of it into a small window.

Choosing the rep counts

This is the part people get wrong, and the whole session depends on it.

The rule: pick a number you can complete in about forty seconds when fresh, which usually means roughly half of your maximum for that exercise. If you can do twenty push-ups, use eight to ten per minute, not fifteen.

This feels far too easy for the first three minutes. That is correct and it is the entire design. By minute eight the same number is genuinely hard, and by minute twelve it is near your limit. An EMOM that is hard in minute two will collapse by minute six and you will spend the second half failing sets, which trains nothing useful.

The forty-second rule

If a set takes longer than forty-five seconds when you are fresh, the rep count is too high. If it takes under twenty seconds in the final minute, it was too low. Adjust next session rather than mid-session — changing the number partway through destroys the comparison that makes the format useful.

Three EMOM sessions

Session 1 — Full body, 16 minutes

Alternate four exercises, one per minute, four rounds:

  • Minute 1: Bodyweight squats × 15
  • Minute 2: Push-ups × 8–10
  • Minute 3: Reverse lunges × 10 total
  • Minute 4: Inverted rows × 8–10

The most useful default. Alternating regions means each muscle group gets three minutes of recovery between its sets, which is why this can be sustained for sixteen minutes.

Session 2 — Upper body, 12 minutes

Alternate two exercises, six rounds each: push-ups × 8 on odd minutes, inverted rows or pull-ups × 5–6 on even minutes. Higher density, more fatiguing, and a good use of a short window if pulling is your priority — the setup options are in rows at home.

Session 3 — Lower body and core, 14 minutes

  • Odd minutes: Bulgarian split squats × 8 each leg, alternating legs each round
  • Even minutes: Dead bugs × 10 each side

Split squats are demanding enough that eight per leg at minute twelve is a genuine effort. Setup detail in Bulgarian split squats at home.

How to progress an EMOM

Four levers, in the order they should be used:

Extend the duration. Twelve minutes to sixteen to twenty. The simplest progression and the one to use first, because it increases volume without changing anything else.

Add repetitions. One more per minute. A small change that compounds — one extra rep across sixteen minutes is sixteen extra repetitions.

Harder variations. Once you are at the top of a sensible duration, switch to a harder version of the movement and drop the rep count back down. The ladders are in bodyweight exercise progressions.

Reduce the exercises in rotation. Going from four alternating exercises to two means each muscle group gets one minute of recovery instead of three. This is a large jump in difficulty — save it for last.

Where people go wrong

Choosing rep counts by ego

The most common failure by a wide margin. Half your maximum feels insultingly easy in minute one, so people pick two-thirds, and the session falls apart in the second half.

Turning it into a race

Rushing the repetitions to buy more rest converts an EMOM into a conditioning session with degraded form. Move at a controlled tempo and accept the rest you earn.

Changing the numbers every session

The format's main advantage is comparability. If the rep count and duration change weekly, you lose the ability to tell whether anything is improving — which is most of the point. Hold both fixed for at least four weeks.

Using it for skill work

EMOM is a volume format, not a skill format. Practising a hard progression under a clock and accumulating fatigue is a good way to groove poor technique.

Frequently asked questions

What does EMOM mean in a workout?

Every Minute On the Minute. You begin a set at the start of each minute, complete the prescribed repetitions, and rest for whatever remains of that minute before the next one begins.

How many reps should I do in an EMOM?

A number you can complete in about forty seconds when fresh, which is usually around half of your maximum for that exercise. It will feel far too easy for the first few minutes, which is exactly the intention.

How long should an EMOM workout be?

Twelve to twenty minutes for most home sessions. Below twelve there is not enough accumulated fatigue for the format to do its work; beyond twenty, form usually degrades faster than the extra volume is worth.

Is EMOM good for building muscle?

It can be. It accumulates substantial weekly volume in a short window and enforces consistent rest, both of which support growth. It is less suited to maximum strength work, where longer rest between fewer, harder sets is preferable.

Can you do EMOM workouts every day?

Not at high intensity. EMOM sessions accumulate real training volume and need recovery like any other resistance training. Two or three a week, on non-consecutive days, is a sensible ceiling.

What happens if I cannot finish the reps in a minute?

Stop the set at the end of the minute and start the next one on schedule with the same target. If you miss the target on two consecutive minutes, the rep count was too high — note it and reduce it next session rather than mid-session.
