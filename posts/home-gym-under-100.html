Home Gym Setup Under $100: A Complete Shopping List

Four items, none redundant, covering every movement pattern with room in the budget. And a strong argument for spending nothing at all for the first two months.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

What's in this guide

  1. Spend nothing for two months first
  2. The list
  3. Resistance bands
  4. Doorway pull-up bar
  5. Foam tiles
  6. Furniture sliders
  7. What this covers
  8. What to add next

Four items cover every movement pattern a home trainee needs, cost well under a hundred pounds or dollars in total, and store in a corner. Prices vary by market and over time, so treat the ordering as the advice and the totals as indicative.

Spend nothing for two months first

Before the list, the most useful buying advice on this page.

Equipment does not create consistency. The failure mode in home training is almost always that the routine stopped, not that the equipment was inadequate, and buying things is a very effective way of feeling like you have started without having started.

Train with nothing for two months. Pushing, squatting, hinging and bracing all work with a floor; pulling can be covered temporarily by a towel on a door handle or rows under a table — see rows at home. After two months you will know which gap you actually hit rather than which one you imagined.

It is almost always pulling. Which is why the list starts there.

The list

A four-item home gym shopping list with what each unlocks
ItemUnlocksPriority
Resistance band setPulling, overhead pressing, assistance1st
Doorway pull-up barRows, pull-ups, hanging2nd
Foam tiles (4–6)Comfort, noise reduction, coverage3rd
Furniture slidersHamstring curls, sliding core work4th

Nothing here is redundant with anything else, which is the point. A common mistake is buying several items that solve the same problem — a suspension trainer, a pull-up bar and bands all address pulling, and you need one of them, not three.

Resistance bands

The first purchase, and the one to make if you only make one. Bands are the only item that works in every home regardless of door frames, floor type, ceiling height or tenancy terms.

They solve pulling — the one genuine gap in bodyweight training — and additionally load overhead pressing, lateral raises, face pulls and direct arm work, none of which bodyweight reaches well.

Buy: a set with at least three strengths rather than a single band, plus a door anchor. Long loop bands are the more versatile choice because they also assist pull-ups; tube bands with handles are more comfortable for pressing. The comparison is in loop bands vs tube bands, and a full session in the resistance band full-body workout.

Doorway pull-up bar

The second purchase, if a suitable door frame exists — and plenty of homes have none, so check before budgeting for it.

A bar unlocks hanging, rows at any angle, and eventually pull-ups, which is the single most satisfying strength milestone available at home — the route is in how to do your first pull-up at home.

Buy: a leverage-mount bar, never a telescopic pressure-fit one. Check the rated capacity against your bodyweight with a real margin. Fit, frame suitability and how to avoid crushing the wall above the doorway are covered in the doorway pull-up bar guide.

Foam tiles

Third, and only if you train on a hard floor or above a neighbour. On carpet with underlay you can skip this entirely and put the money elsewhere.

Four to six interlocking EVA tiles cover roughly two metres by one, which is the area a full session actually uses — including under your hands, which is the coverage mistake almost everyone makes.

Buy: density over thickness. If you can compress it flat between finger and thumb, your bodyweight will do the same. Around 12 to 15 millimetres is the sensible target. Children's play tiles are frequently the same material at lower prices — the comparison is in yoga mat vs exercise mat vs puzzle tiles.

Furniture sliders

Fourth, and the cheapest item by a wide margin. They unlock sliding hamstring curls, which train knee flexion — a function that home leg training otherwise misses almost entirely, since squats and lunges are all knee-dominant.

Buy: felt-bottomed for hard floors, hard plastic for carpet. Furniture-moving packs usually include both and cost less than fitness-branded versions. The exercises are in furniture sliders for core and leg training.

The common mistake is not buying too little. It is buying three things that solve the same problem.

What this covers

All five movement patterns, at difficulties ranging from beginner to advanced:

  • Squat — bodyweight and single-leg progressions, free.
  • Hinge — glute bridges free, band Romanian deadlifts, slider hamstring curls.
  • Push — push-up progressions free, band overhead press.
  • Pull — band rows, bar rows, pull-ups.
  • Brace — planks and dead bugs free, slider body saws.

That is a complete programme. Adult activity guidance asks for muscle-strengthening activity involving all major muscle groups on two or more days a week,1 and this clears it comfortably. Light loads taken close to failure produce comparable muscle growth to heavy ones,2 so the modest resistance is not the limitation people assume.

The programme to run with it is the three-day split, or the 4-week beginner plan if you are starting out.

What to add next

Only once something has clearly become the limiting factor.

An adjustable dumbbell when single-leg bodyweight work is no longer challenging — see the adjustable dumbbells buying guide.

A weighted vest when push-ups and pull-ups have outgrown bodyweight, though a loaded rucksack does this free — see weighted vest training and DIY home gym equipment.

A suspension trainer or rings if you want continuously adjustable rowing and suspended leg work — see suspension trainers at home.

What not to add: a bench, until you own dumbbells and chest work is a priority; and anything that duplicates what you already have. The full reasoning is in the minimalist home gym guide.

If the budget is tighter than $100

The list degrades gracefully, which is one of its better properties. At each budget level you lose capability rather than losing the ability to train.

Around a third of the budget: resistance bands and furniture sliders. That covers pulling, overhead pressing and hamstring work — the three genuine gaps in equipment-free training — and leaves comfort and noise unsolved. If you are on carpet with nobody below you, this is arguably the complete list anyway.

Around two-thirds: add the doorway bar. Now you have hanging and pull-ups, which is the largest single capability jump on the list.

Almost nothing: a towel and a sturdy table. The towel covers rows on a door handle and doubles as a slider on a hard floor; the table covers inverted rows properly. Neither costs anything and between them they close most of the pulling gap — see DIY home gym equipment.

Buying second-hand is worth considering for the pull-up bar, where condition is easy to assess and steel does not wear out. It is worth avoiding for bands, since latex degrades with age and UV and you cannot tell how a used set has been stored — a band failing under tension recoils toward your face.

Frequently asked questions

What should I buy for a home gym under $100?

Resistance bands first, a doorway pull-up bar second if a suitable frame exists, four to six foam tiles third if you train on a hard floor, and furniture sliders fourth. Nothing on that list duplicates anything else.

What is the first thing to buy for a home gym?

Resistance bands. They solve pulling, which is the one movement pattern bodyweight training cannot supply, and they work in every home regardless of door frames, floor type, ceiling height or tenancy terms.

Do I need equipment to start training at home?

No, and you should train with nothing for two months first. Pushing, squatting, hinging and bracing all work with a floor, and pulling can be covered temporarily by a towel on a door handle or rows under a table.

Is $100 enough for a home gym?

Comfortably, for a complete bodyweight-based programme covering all five movement patterns. The limitation is heavy lower-body work, which needs added load — and that only becomes relevant after a year or more of consistent training.

What home gym equipment is redundant?

Anything solving a problem you have already solved. A suspension trainer, a pull-up bar and resistance bands all address pulling; you need one of them, not three. That is the most common way people overspend.
