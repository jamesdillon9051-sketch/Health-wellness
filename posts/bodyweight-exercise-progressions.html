Bodyweight Exercise Progressions: Beginner to Advanced

Every ladder, in order, with a test for each rung. The point of a progression is not the list of exercises — it is knowing exactly when you have earned the next one.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

A progression is only useful if it comes with a test. “Do knee push-ups until you can do a real one” is not a progression — it names two points and leaves out everything in between, including how you would know you had arrived.

What follows is every ladder for every movement pattern, with a specific criterion for each rung. Work them in order.

The graduation rule

Move up when you can complete three sets of the target repetitions with good form and roughly two repetitions still in reserve, on two separate sessions.

All three parts matter. The rep target ensures the current stage is genuinely comfortable rather than barely survivable. The reps in reserve stop you progressing on the back of a maximal effort you cannot repeat. And requiring two sessions rules out the good day that flatters you into moving on too early.

Progressing before you meet the criterion is the single most common reason people stall, because the next rung is then too hard to accumulate any volume on, and they spend months failing at it rather than weeks succeeding at the one below.

The five levers

Every progression below is built from the same five adjustments, and it is worth recognising them as you go:

  1. Proximity to failure — free, instant, most neglected.
  2. Tempo — a three-second descent, a two-second pause.
  3. Range of motion — deeper, or with an elevated hand or foot position.
  4. Leverage — changing the angle so more of your bodyweight loads the working muscle.
  5. Limbs — from two to one.

Evidence comparing light and heavy loads found comparable hypertrophy when sets were taken close to failure,1 which is why lever one belongs at the top of that list rather than being treated as an afterthought.

The ladder is not the hard part. Staying on a rung long enough to earn the next one is.

Why the graduation criteria are what they are

The rep targets in the tables above are not arbitrary, and understanding where they come from makes them easier to trust when a stage feels tedious.

Three sets is the minimum at which a working set count starts to matter. Weekly volume drives adaptation in a dose-response fashion,2 and a single set of a movement twice a week is unlikely to accumulate enough. Three sets across two or three sessions puts most patterns in a productive weekly range without requiring long sessions.

The target repetitions vary by pattern for a mechanical reason. Push-up and row targets sit around twelve because at that point the exercise has drifted from a strength stimulus toward endurance, and the sensible response is to make the movement harder rather than to keep adding repetitions. Squat targets sit higher — fifteen or twenty — because two-legged lower-body movements are so light relative to your capacity that twelve repetitions is barely a warm-up. Plank targets are in seconds because they are isometric, and the ceiling arrives around sixty seconds for the reasons set out in the plank progression chart.

The two-repetitions-in-reserve requirement is the part people most often ignore, and it is doing important work. If you can only hit the target by grinding out a maximal set, you cannot repeat that performance for three sets, and you certainly cannot repeat it at the next rung. Progressing on the back of a single best-ever effort is how people end up stuck at a stage they were never actually ready for.

Regressions: going backwards on purpose

Every ladder here runs in both directions, and moving down is a normal training decision rather than an admission of failure. Three situations where it is the correct response.

After a break. Detraining is real, and two or three weeks away costs more at the harder rungs than the easier ones. Coming back from a fortnight off, drop one rung for the first week and return to where you were in the second. Trying to resume at your previous stage usually produces a poor session, disproportionate soreness, and a second missed week — the sequence covered in how to restart after missing two weeks.

When form degrades mid-set. If your hips sag on repetition six of ten, the working set effectively ended at five. Rather than completing four bad repetitions, drop to the rung below and complete ten good ones. The stimulus from a controlled easier variation is greater than from a collapsing harder one, and the injury risk is lower.

During a hard period of life. Poor sleep, high stress and reduced food intake all lower what you can recover from. Sleep in particular has documented effects on training performance,5 and a fortnight of four-hour nights is not the moment to attempt a new rung. Dropping a stage and maintaining is a far better outcome than failing at your current stage and stopping altogether.

What these ladders do not cover

Two honest gaps worth naming, so you are not left wondering why the ladder ran out.

The lower-body ladders top out sooner than the upper-body ones. A one-arm push-up loads roughly your whole bodyweight through one arm, which for most people is a genuinely heavy stimulus. A pistol squat loads your bodyweight through one leg — but your legs are far stronger relative to your bodyweight than your arms are, so the same arrangement is proportionally much lighter. This is the clearest structural limitation of bodyweight training and the reason the squat and hinge ladders have fewer meaningful rungs than the push ladder.

The top rungs become skill-limited rather than strength-limited. An archer push-up requires trunk anti-rotation strength and shoulder stability that may become the constraint before your chest does. A pistol squat depends heavily on ankle range. At that point you are no longer training the muscle you set out to train, and the honest response is to add a small amount of external load to an earlier rung instead — a loaded rucksack or a weighted vest, covered in DIY home gym equipment and weighted vest training.

Neither gap arrives quickly. For most people they sit one to three years of consistent training away, and the fuller assessment is in can you build muscle with bodyweight only.

How to use these ladders

Pick one exercise per pattern. A session should contain one push, one pull, one squat, one hinge and one brace — not three variations of each. The structure is in how to structure a home workout.

You will be at different stages in different patterns. That is normal and expected. Being at stage 8 in push and stage 3 in pull is extremely common, because pulling requires equipment and gets under-trained.

Write down your stage. Progress at this pace is invisible without a record — six weeks of improvement looks like nothing from the inside. Tracking progress without a gym or scale covers what to note.

Do not skip rungs. The graduation criteria exist because each rung builds something the next depends on. Skipping produces the stall described below.

Give each rung six weeks minimum. Weekly volume drives adaptation,2 and accumulating enough of it at a given difficulty takes longer than the enthusiasm of a new stage suggests.

When you stall

Stalling for more than four weeks at a rung usually has one of five causes, in rough order of likelihood.

You progressed too early. Drop back one rung, meet the criterion properly, and come back. Uncomfortable, and the fix about half the time.

The jump is too large. Some gaps between rungs are wider than others, particularly floor push-up to feet-elevated. Insert an intermediate step — stairs are useful precisely because they give increments furniture does not.

You are not close enough to failure. With a fixed load, effort is the intensity variable.3 If your sets end at discomfort rather than near failure, no amount of programme adjustment will help.

Volume or frequency is too low. Two hard sets once a week will not progress most patterns. Two or three sessions a week per pattern is the target, and the frequency evidence supports spreading volume rather than concentrating it.4

Sleep or protein. Less interesting than a programming fix and more often the real answer. Both have documented effects on training outcomes,5 and a stall during a period of poor sleep usually resolves when the sleep does.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know when to progress a bodyweight exercise?

When you can complete three sets of the target repetitions with good form and about two repetitions still in reserve, on two separate sessions. Requiring two sessions prevents a single good day from flattering you into progressing early.

What are the levers for progressing bodyweight exercises?

Five: getting closer to failure, slowing the tempo, increasing range of motion, changing leverage so more bodyweight loads the working muscle, and reducing the number of limbs sharing the load.

How long should I stay at each progression stage?

At least six weeks. Accumulating enough weekly volume at a given difficulty takes longer than the enthusiasm of reaching a new stage suggests, and progressing early is the most common reason people stall.

Why am I stuck on the same bodyweight exercise?

Most often because you progressed before meeting the criterion for the previous rung. Other causes are a jump that is too large, sets that never approach failure, insufficient weekly volume, or inadequate sleep and protein.

Should I be at the same stage for every exercise?

No, and almost nobody is. Being advanced in pushing and early in pulling is very common, because pulling requires something to pull against and therefore gets under-trained at home.

Can I skip stages in a progression?

Not usefully. Each rung builds something the next depends on, and skipping generally produces a stall where you cannot accumulate enough volume at the harder variation to progress at all.
