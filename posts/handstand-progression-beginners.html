Handstand Progression for Beginners at Home

A handstand is a balance skill built on a strength base, practised daily in short doses. The people who get there quickly are not stronger — they practise more often for less time.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

  3. The five stages

A handstand looks like a strength feat and is mostly a balance skill. Plenty of people strong enough to hold one cannot, and plenty of people who can hold one are not especially strong.

What separates people who learn it from people who do not is almost entirely practice frequency. Short, frequent, low-fatigue practice beats long occasional sessions by a wide margin, because balance is a motor skill and motor skills consolidate with repetition rather than with effort.

The strength base you need first

Before starting, you should comfortably be able to hold a plank for a minute, do ten controlled pike push-ups, and hold a chest-to-wall position for thirty seconds without your lower back arching badly.

If pike push-ups are not there yet, spend six weeks on the bodyweight shoulder workout first. The shoulders need to tolerate holding your bodyweight overhead, and rushing that is the main route to wrist and shoulder complaints.

Before you start

Handstand training involves being inverted and occasionally falling. Do not attempt it if you have uncontrolled high blood pressure, a history of shoulder dislocation, wrist injury, a recent head injury, or any condition affecting balance or blood pressure regulation. If you are unsure, ask your doctor first. Clear the area of furniture, and never practise on a hard floor without knowing how to bail.

Bailing safely

Learn this before anything else. Falling out of a handstand badly is the main injury risk, and it is entirely avoidable.

The cartwheel bail. If you tip over past vertical, turn your body to one side, let one hand leave the floor, and step down sideways as though completing a cartwheel. Practise this deliberately from a wall handstand twenty or thirty times before you attempt anything freestanding. It needs to be automatic, because you will not have time to think.

The pike-out. If you are tipping back toward your feet, simply pike at the hips and step down the way you came. Easier and the more common bail from a wall position.

The five stages

Stage 1 — Wrist preparation and plank. Two weeks. Kneel and place your palms on the floor with fingers pointing toward your knees, gently leaning back to stretch the forearms. Then fingers forward, rocking gently side to side. Two minutes, before every session. Wrists take more load in a handstand than most people expect and are the most common early complaint.

Stage 2 — Wall walk. Start in a push-up position with feet against the wall, walk your feet up while walking your hands toward the wall, until your chest is close to it. Hold ten to twenty seconds, walk back down. Three to five repetitions.

This is the single most valuable drill, because it puts you in a genuine handstand line while the wall handles the balance.

Stage 3 — Chest-to-wall hold. The same end position, held for time. Build to three sets of forty-five seconds. Focus on the shape: ribs pulled down, glutes squeezed, legs straight, pushing hard through the shoulders so your ears are behind your arms.

This shape is the whole thing. A handstand held in a bad shape is much harder to balance than one held in a good shape.

Stage 4 — Heel pulls. From the chest-to-wall position, peel one heel off the wall, then the other, and try to hold the balance for a second before touching back. This is where you actually learn to balance, and it is the stage that takes longest.

The correction comes from your fingers: press the fingertips into the floor to stop yourself tipping over, and let the heels return to the wall if you tip back.

Stage 5 — Freestanding. Kick up in an open space with a clear bail path. Aim for consistency of the kick-up before duration — being able to arrive reliably in a controlled position matters more than holding for longer.

Ten minutes a day, six days a week, beats an hour on Sunday. This is a skill, and skills consolidate through repetition rather than effort.

How to practise

Ten to fifteen minutes, five or six days a week, ideally at the start of a session or as a standalone. Stop well before you are fatigued: practising a balance skill tired teaches your nervous system to do it badly, and it also raises the risk of a poor fall.

This is the opposite of how you would programme a strength exercise, and it is why handstand work does not fit neatly into a normal training split. Treat it as skill practice that happens alongside training rather than as part of it.

Keep doing pike push-ups twice a week for the pressing strength. They complement handstand practice without competing with it.

A realistic timeline

Typical time at each handstand stage with near-daily practice
StageTypical duration
Wrist prep and base strength2–4 weeks
Wall walks3–6 weeks
Chest-to-wall holds4–8 weeks
Heel pulls8–16 weeks
Freestanding, a few seconds4–12 weeks

Six months to a year for a reliable freestanding handstand, with near-daily practice. Adults learning this from scratch generally take longer than the internet suggests, and being told six weeks is the main reason people give up at the heel-pull stage.

Common mistakes

Practising only when fresh and motivated. Frequency matters more than intensity here. Five short sessions beat one long one.

Back-to-wall instead of chest-to-wall. Facing away from the wall lets you arch your back and stand at an angle, which teaches a shape you then have to unlearn. Chest-to-wall is harder and correct.

Skipping wrist preparation. Wrists are the most common early complaint and two minutes of preparation prevents most of it.

Not learning to bail. Cheap insurance against the one genuinely risky part of this.

Chasing duration too early. Ten reliable three-second holds build the skill faster than one lucky twenty-second hold.

Where handstand practice fits in a training week

Handstand work sits awkwardly alongside normal training, because it follows different rules. Strength training works through weekly volume accumulated in a few hard sessions;2 skill practice works through frequent low-fatigue repetition. Trying to run the two on the same schedule usually compromises both.

The arrangement that works is to treat handstand practice as something that happens before training rather than within it. Ten minutes of wall work while you are fresh, then your actual session. On non-training days, the ten minutes happens on its own.

Two practical consequences. First, do not put handstand practice at the end of an upper-body session — the shoulders will be fatigued and the practice will be poor quality and less safe. Second, it does not need to be counted against your weekly training volume in the way a hard set does, because it is deliberately kept short of fatigue. Adult activity guidance is met by your resistance sessions,1 and this sits alongside them.

If your week is already full, this is one of the few things that genuinely can be added without subtracting something else — provided you keep it short. Where it does compete is with pressing volume, so if your shoulders feel beaten up, reduce the pike push-ups before you reduce the practice. How to think about total weekly load is covered in how many days a week should you work out, and the scheduling side in the weekly home workout schedule.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to learn a handstand?

Six months to a year for a reliable freestanding hold, with near-daily practice. Adults starting from scratch usually take longer than online claims suggest, and unrealistic expectations are the main reason people abandon it at the heel-pull stage.

What is the best handstand drill for beginners?

Chest-to-wall holds. They put you in a genuine handstand line while the wall handles the balance, so you can build both the shape and the shoulder endurance before adding the balance element.

Should I face the wall or away from it?

Chest to wall. Facing away lets you arch your back and hold the body at an angle, which teaches a shape you then have to unlearn. Chest to wall is harder and correct.

How often should I practise handstands?

Ten to fifteen minutes, five or six days a week, stopping well before fatigue. Balance is a motor skill that consolidates through frequent repetition rather than through hard effort.

How do I fall safely out of a handstand?

Learn the cartwheel bail: if you tip past vertical, turn to one side, let one hand leave the floor and step down sideways. Practise it deliberately twenty or thirty times from a wall handstand before attempting anything freestanding.

Do I need to be strong to do a handstand?

You need a base — a one-minute plank, ten controlled pike push-ups, and a thirty-second chest-to-wall hold. Beyond that, a handstand is mostly a balance skill, and plenty of strong people cannot hold one.
