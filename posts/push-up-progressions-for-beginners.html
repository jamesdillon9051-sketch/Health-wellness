Push-Up Progressions for Absolute Beginners (Wall to Full)

Six stages, each with a specific test for when to move on. The usual advice — do knee push-ups until you can do a real one — skips the part where you find out whether it is working.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

The standard advice for people who cannot do a push-up is to do knee push-ups. It is not wrong exactly, but it has two problems: it changes the movement rather than reducing the load, and it provides no way of knowing when you are ready to progress.

An incline ladder solves both. The movement stays identical throughout — the same body line, the same elbow path — and the only thing that changes is what percentage of your bodyweight you are pressing. Each stage has a specific test for moving on.

Why a ladder beats knee push-ups

A knee push-up shortens the lever by moving the pivot point from your toes to your knees. That reduces the load, but it also removes the trunk requirement almost entirely — and holding a rigid line from head to heel is a substantial part of what a push-up trains.

An incline push-up reduces load by changing the angle rather than the body position. You keep the full-body tension, the same joint angles and the same movement path. When you eventually reach the floor, nothing about the exercise is unfamiliar.

Knee push-ups are not useless — they are a reasonable option if you have no suitable surface. But given a wall, a counter and a chair, the incline route is better.

The graduation rule

Move to the next stage when you can complete three sets of twelve repetitions with good form and two repetitions still in reserve, on two separate sessions.

Three parts of that matter. Twelve gives enough volume that the stage is genuinely comfortable. Two in reserve means you are not scraping through. And two separate sessions rules out the good day that flatters you into progressing early.

Form here means the standard set out in how to do a perfect push-up: straight line from ear to ankle, elbows at roughly forty-five degrees, chest reaching within an inch of the surface. If the hips sag on repetition nine, you have not met the criterion.

Expect this to take months, not weeks

A complete beginner might spend three to five weeks at each of the first few stages. Reaching a full floor push-up in four to six months from a standing start is a good outcome, not a slow one. Rushing the ladder is the most common reason people stall at stage five.

Stage 1: wall push-up

Stand about arm's length from a wall, hands slightly wider than shoulders at chest height. Lean in until your chest is close to the wall, then press back. Move your feet further from the wall to make it harder.

This stage is about learning the body line and the elbow path with almost no load. If you can already do twelve of these comfortably, start at stage two.

Stage 2: counter push-up

Hands on a kitchen counter or a sturdy desk, roughly hip height. Walk your feet back until your body is at a comfortable angle with the line held from ear to ankle.

This is where most beginners should start, and where the trunk requirement becomes real. Check that the counter is fixed and will not slide.

Stage 3: chair or sofa push-up

Hands on the arm of a sofa, the seat of a sturdy chair, or a low windowsill — roughly knee to mid-thigh height. A significant jump from the counter, so expect your repetitions to drop sharply at first.

Make sure whatever you use cannot tip. A dining chair with your hands on the seat is fine; a chair with your hands on the back is not.

Stage 4: stair push-up

Hands on the third or fourth step of a staircase. The advantage here is granularity: as you get stronger, move down one step at a time, which gives you four or five micro-progressions instead of one large jump.

If you have stairs, this stage does most of the work of getting you to the floor. It is also worth knowing about for other reasons — see stair workouts.

Stage 5: negative floor push-up

From the top of a floor push-up, lower yourself as slowly as you can — aim for four to five seconds — until your chest touches. Then get back to the top however you need to: drop to your knees, push up, and reset.

Lowering under control is possible at a load you cannot yet press, which makes negatives the bridge between the last incline and the full movement. Three sets of five slow negatives is a session.

This stage is more fatiguing than it looks and is the one where people most often overdo it. Two sessions a week is plenty.

Stage 6: full floor push-up

The real thing. When you first get here you may manage one or two, and the sensible approach is to do several sets of one or two rather than one set of two and stopping.

Once you can do three sets of twelve on the floor, the ladder continues upward: feet elevated, then archer push-ups, then eventually one-arm progressions. Those are covered in how to build chest muscle without weights.

How to programme it

Two to three sessions a week, three sets each, with at least a day between. Weekly volume drives adaptation,1 so three sessions of three sets beats one session of nine.

Stop each set two repetitions short of failure except the last, which can go closer. Training close to failure is what produces adaptation when the load is light,2 but taking every set to absolute failure costs recovery disproportionate to the benefit.3

Write down the stage and the repetitions every session. Without a record, the graduation criterion is guesswork — and this is exactly the kind of slow progress that is invisible without numbers, as discussed in tracking progress without a gym or scale.

If you get stuck

Stalling for more than three or four weeks at one stage usually has one of four causes.

The jump was too large. Find an intermediate surface. This is why stairs are useful — they give you increments the furniture in your house does not.

You are training too often. Push-ups every day is a common instinct and it prevents the recovery in which adaptation happens. Three sessions a week is the ceiling for most people.

The trunk is the limiting factor, not the chest. If the hips sag before the arms give out, the plank capacity is what needs work. Add the plank progression and revisit.

Sleep or food. Less exciting than a programming fix and more often the actual answer. Sleep has documented effects on training performance and recovery,4 and a stalled progression during a period of poor sleep usually resolves when the sleep does.

Frequently asked questions

How do beginners progress to a full push-up?

Through an incline ladder: wall, counter, chair or sofa, stairs, slow negatives on the floor, then the full movement. Each stage keeps the movement identical and only changes what percentage of your bodyweight you press.

Are knee push-ups good for beginners?

They are acceptable but inferior to incline push-ups. Dropping to the knees removes almost all of the trunk requirement, which is a substantial part of what a push-up trains. Incline push-ups reduce load while keeping the full body line.

How long does it take to do a full push-up?

Four to six months from a genuine standing start is a good outcome. A complete beginner might spend three to five weeks at each of the early stages. Progressing faster than that is usually a sign of skipping the graduation criteria.

When should I move to the next push-up stage?

When you can complete three sets of twelve with good form and two repetitions still in reserve, on two separate sessions. Requiring two sessions rules out a single good day flattering you into progressing early.

How often should I do push-ups?

Two to three sessions a week with at least a day between them. Daily push-ups is a common instinct that prevents the recovery in which adaptation actually happens.

Why am I stuck at the same push-up stage?

Usually one of four things: the jump between surfaces was too large, you are training too frequently, your trunk rather than your chest is the limiting factor, or your sleep and food are inadequate. Check them in that order.
