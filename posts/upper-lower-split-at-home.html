Upper/Lower Split for Home Training

If you can train four times a week, upper/lower is probably the best structure available at home. It hits everything twice, keeps sessions short, and survives a missed day.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

What's in this guide

  3. Upper body session
  4. Lower body session

Upper/lower splits the week into two session types and alternates them. On four days a week that means every muscle group is trained twice — the frequency the evidence tends to favour — while each session stays short enough to finish in half an hour.

It is the structure I would recommend to most people who have got past the beginner stage and can reliably find four evenings.

Why upper/lower works well at home

Three reasons.

Frequency without long sessions. A meta-analysis found that when weekly volume is equated, training a muscle group more frequently produces similar or slightly better hypertrophy.1 Four days of upper/lower gives you twice-weekly frequency for everything without any session exceeding about thirty-five minutes.

It degrades gracefully. Miss one of four sessions and you still have a complete week: one upper, one lower, plus whichever you managed twice. Miss one of three full-body sessions and you have lost a third of your training.

Equipment is spread evenly. Unlike push/pull/legs, the pulling problem does not concentrate into a single session that becomes impossible without a bar.

The weekly schedule

Four-day upper lower weekly schedule
DaySession
MondayUpper A
TuesdayLower A
WednesdayRest or walk
ThursdayUpper B
FridayLower B
Sat / SunRest

Two on, one off, two on, two off. You can also run it Monday/Tuesday/Thursday/Saturday or any pattern that avoids four consecutive training days.

Upper body session

Alternate a pushing and a pulling movement to halve the dead time, as described in how to structure a home workout.

  • Pull-up or inverted row — 4 × as many as possible
  • Push-up variation — 4 × 8–15
  • Pike push-up — 3 × 6–12
  • Band or towel row — 3 × 12–20
  • Chair dip — 2 × 10–15
  • Band pull-apart — 2 × 15–25

Lower body session

  • Bulgarian split squat — 4 × 8–12 each leg
  • Slow-descent squat — 3 × 15–25
  • Single-leg glute bridge or hip thrust — 3 × 10–15 each side
  • Reverse lunge — 3 × 10–12 each leg
  • Single-leg calf raise — 3 × 15–25
  • Dead bug or hollow hold — 3 sets

Making the two upper days different

You do not have to, but varying emphasis between the A and B sessions keeps things interesting and spreads stress across joints.

A simple approach: make the A sessions emphasise the harder, lower-rep variations and the B sessions emphasise higher repetitions with easier variations. So Upper A leads with pull-ups and decline push-ups; Upper B leads with inverted rows and standard push-ups for higher reps. Lower A leads with split squats; Lower B leads with higher-rep squats and glute work.

This is not essential. Running the identical session twice a week is perfectly effective and simpler to track.

Upper/lower vs full body vs PPL

Comparison of home training splits by weekly frequency
Days availableBest structureWhy
2Full bodyOnly way to hit everything twice
3Full bodyThree exposures per muscle group per week
4Upper / lowerTwice-weekly frequency, short sessions
5–6Push / pull / legsEnough days to hit everything twice with more focus

The pattern is simple: choose the split that gets each muscle group trained about twice a week given the number of days you actually have. At four days that is upper/lower; at three it is full body; at six it is PPL.

Choose for the week you have, not the week you want

The most common planning error is picking a split for an idealised schedule. Four sessions attempted and two completed is worse than three sessions attempted and three completed, because the second gives you a plan you can actually evaluate.

Progressing it

Run the same sessions for eight weeks. Add a set to the first two exercises around week three, start taking final sets close to failure around week five, and move to harder variations around week seven where you are at the top of a rep range.

Weekly volume drives growth up to a point,2 so adding sets is a legitimate progression — but there is a ceiling, and past roughly twenty hard sets per muscle group per week most people are adding fatigue rather than stimulus. When adding sets stops helping, make the movements harder instead using the progression ladder.

What a real week actually looks like

The schedule table above is the plan. What it looks like in practice is worth describing honestly, because the gap between the two is where most routines die.

A good week is four completed sessions of thirty to thirty-five minutes. A normal week is three completed sessions and one that got moved to the following day or dropped. A bad week is two. The plan is built so that a normal week still trains every muscle group at least once and most of them twice, which is why the sessions alternate rather than stacking all the upper work together.

The practical advice that follows from this: when a session has to go, drop the second of the pair rather than the first. Losing Lower B still leaves you with a lower body session that week. Losing Lower A and then also missing Lower B because the week ran away leaves you with none, and that is the pattern that compounds.

The three-day version

If four days turns out to be optimistic, you can run upper/lower on three by simply alternating and letting the pattern roll across weeks: upper, lower, upper in week one; lower, upper, lower in week two. Each muscle group then gets trained about once every five days, averaging out at roughly 1.5 times a week.

This works, but at three days a week a full-body structure hits each muscle group three times rather than 1.5, for the same total training time. Given that frequency appears to be at least mildly beneficial when volume is equated,1 the three-day full-body split is the better choice if three is genuinely your number. Use the rolling upper/lower version only if you strongly prefer shorter sessions.

How to know it is working

Four days a week of hard training should produce visible changes in the numbers within about six weeks. The things worth watching, in order of usefulness:

Reps on the first exercise of each session. This is the cleanest signal because it is measured when you are freshest and least affected by how the rest of the session went. If your first-exercise pull-up or split squat numbers are not moving over six weeks at the same variation and tempo, something outside the plan is limiting you.

How the last set feels. More subjective, but useful. If sets that used to require full effort now finish comfortably, you have adapted and it is time to progress the variation rather than add another rep.

Recovery between sessions. Persistent soreness that is still present when the next session of that type arrives usually means the volume has outrun your recovery, not that the plan is working especially well. Soreness is a poor proxy for progress, as covered in DOMS: why you're sore and what actually helps.

What is not worth watching weekly: the scale, and the mirror. Both move too slowly and too noisily to tell you anything about a four-week block. Tracking progress without a gym or a scale covers what to record instead.

Where to go next

If four days is not realistic, drop to the three-day split rather than running this one badly. If you need to solve pulling first, start with rows at home.

Frequently asked questions

Is an upper/lower split good for home workouts?

It is arguably the best structure available if you can train four days a week. It gives every muscle group twice-weekly frequency, keeps individual sessions to about thirty-five minutes, and spreads equipment needs evenly across sessions.

How many days a week is upper/lower?

Four is the standard and the most effective, giving two upper and two lower sessions. It can be run on three days by alternating, but each muscle group then gets trained about once every five days.

Should upper A and upper B be different?

They do not need to be. Varying emphasis — harder lower-rep work on one day, higher-rep work on the other — spreads joint stress and adds variety, but running identical sessions twice a week is effective and easier to track.

Is upper/lower better than full body?

It depends entirely on how many days you train. At two or three days a week full body hits each muscle group more often. At four days upper/lower is usually the better structure.

Can I do upper/lower with no equipment?

Mostly. The pushing, leg and core work needs nothing. The pulling exercises need a bar, bands or a sturdy table, and skipping them leaves a significant gap in the plan.
