How to Train Legs at Home Without Any Equipment

The problem with bodyweight leg training is not a lack of exercises. It is that two legs sharing your bodyweight stops being a load, and most sessions never solve that.

SR By Sam Reyes Published 29 August 2026 · Last updated 29 August 2026

Search for a no-equipment leg workout and you will find lists of twenty exercises, most of them variations on a squat, performed for high repetitions. The lists are not wrong so much as beside the point.

The real constraint is load. Two legs sharing your bodyweight is a light load, and once you can perform twenty bodyweight squats you have stopped training strength and started training endurance. Adding a twenty-first repetition does not fix this.

The load problem

Consider what happens with a barbell. You add weight, the exercise stays hard, and you keep adapting. With bodyweight legs there is no weight to add, so the difficulty is capped by your own mass distributed across two limbs.

The available responses are: do more repetitions, slow the movement down, or reduce the number of limbs sharing the load. Only the third genuinely restores a strength stimulus, and it does so dramatically — moving from two legs to one roughly doubles the load per limb.

Evidence comparing light and heavy loads found comparable muscle growth when sets are taken close to failure.1 The catch is that with two-legged squats, reaching close to failure takes forty repetitions and several minutes, by which point the limiting factor is cardiovascular rather than muscular. Single-leg work reaches the same place in eight to twelve repetitions.

The single-leg principle

Almost every exercise in a well-designed home leg session should be single-leg or heavily single-leg biased. This is the one structural decision that separates a session that works from one that merely fills twenty-five minutes.

The secondary benefits are real too: single-leg work trains balance and hip stability, addresses left-right strength differences that two-legged work conceals, and is closer to how legs are actually used.

The session

Warm up for five minutes first. Rest ninety seconds between sets. About thirty minutes total.

  1. Bulgarian split squat — 3–4 × 8–12 each leg

    Rear foot elevated on a low surface. The hardest and most productive exercise available, which is why it leads. Setup is most of the exercise and is covered in Bulgarian split squats at home.

  2. Slow-descent bodyweight squat — 3 × 12–20

    Three seconds down, one second pause, normal speed up. Two-legged, but the tempo restores some of what the light load removes. Form detail in bodyweight squat form.

  3. Single-leg glute bridge — 3 × 10–15 each side

    One-second squeeze at the top of every repetition. The hinge pattern, and the exercise most directly useful to anyone who sits all day.

  4. Reverse lunge — 3 × 10–12 each leg

    Step backwards rather than forwards, which puts less shear through the front knee and is easier to control.

  5. Single-leg calf raise — 3 × 15–25

    Off a step if you have one, for the extra range. Calves respond to high repetitions and are the one place where twenty-five is a reasonable target.

  6. Wall sit — 2 × 45–90 seconds

    An isometric finisher. Optional, and the first thing to cut if time is short.

Why this order

Hardest and most balance-demanding first, while you are freshest. The split squat requires both strength and stability, and doing it after four other exercises means training a degraded version of it.

The two-legged squat sits second deliberately: it is a good exercise but not the primary stimulus, and putting it first would pre-fatigue you for the work that matters more. The general logic is in how to structure a home workout.

Scaling up and down

If this is too hard

  • Hold a wall or doorframe during the split squats and lunges. This is a scaling option, not a failure.
  • Lower the rear foot elevation, or remove it entirely and do a static split squat with both feet on the floor.
  • Two-legged glute bridges instead of single-leg.
  • Two sets instead of three.

If this is too easy

  • Slow every descent to three or four seconds.
  • Add a two-second pause at the bottom of each repetition.
  • Elevate the front foot in the split squat to increase range.
  • Begin working toward a pistol squat, which is the endpoint of the single-leg ladder.
  • Load a rucksack with books and wear it — see DIY home gym equipment.

How often

Twice a week for most people, with at least two days between sessions. Legs are a large amount of muscle and single-leg work is demanding; three hard leg sessions a week is more than most home trainees recover from well.

Weekly volume drives adaptation up to a point,2 and two sessions of the above is roughly fifteen to twenty hard sets per leg per week, which sits comfortably in the productive range.

What goes wrong

Staying on two legs. The central error. A session of squats, jump squats, wall sits and calf raises is a session with no genuine strength stimulus for anyone past their first two months.

Chasing repetition counts. Fifty bodyweight squats is an endurance achievement. It is not evidence that your legs are getting stronger.

Skipping the hinge. Squats and lunges are knee-dominant. Without glute bridges or an equivalent, the posterior chain is undertrained — and that is the half most affected by sitting.

Not resting. Single-leg work is genuinely hard and needs ninety seconds between sets. Running it as a circuit converts it into conditioning.

The single-leg ladder, end to end

Because load is the constraint, it helps to see the whole progression laid out. Each step roughly corresponds to a meaningful increase in difficulty, and you should be able to do three sets of twelve on one before moving to the next.

Progression ladder for single-leg squatting patterns
StageExerciseRoughly equivalent to
1Bodyweight squatHalf your bodyweight per leg
2Static split squat, both feet downAround 60% per front leg
3Reverse lungeAround 70%, plus balance
4Bulgarian split squatAround 85% per front leg
5Elevated-front-foot split squat85% through a longer range
6Assisted pistol squatClose to full bodyweight
7Pistol squatFull bodyweight, one leg

Most people never need to go past stage four or five. A Bulgarian split squat done with a slow descent and a pause, taken close to failure, is a genuinely hard exercise that will keep producing adaptation for a long time. The pistol squat is a skill as much as a strength feat, and if you want it, the route is in the pistol squat progression.

Do not neglect the hinge

Squats and lunges are knee-dominant patterns: the knee bends a lot, the hip bends less, and the quadriceps do most of the work. The hinge pattern is the opposite — the hip travels backward, the knee stays relatively straight, and the glutes and hamstrings do the work.

Without a barbell, the hinge is harder to load at home, which is why it gets skipped. Glute bridges are the accessible option, and single-leg versions with a pause at the top are considerably more demanding than they look. Nordic curl negatives, if you can hook your feet under something solid, are the hardest hamstring exercise available without equipment.

This matters beyond aesthetics. The posterior chain is the half most affected by sitting for long periods, and a leg session made entirely of squats and lunges reinforces the imbalance rather than addressing it. There is more on the glute side specifically in glute exercises at home.

Frequently asked questions

Can you build leg muscle without weights?

Yes, but only if you move to single-leg work. Two legs sharing your bodyweight stops providing a strength stimulus within a couple of months, and adding repetitions trains endurance rather than strength. Single-leg variations roughly double the load per limb.

What is the best no-equipment leg exercise?

The Bulgarian split squat. It places roughly eighty-five per cent of your bodyweight through one leg through a long range, and it also trains balance and hip stability that two-legged squats do not.

How many reps should I do for bodyweight legs?

Eight to fifteen for single-leg exercises, twelve to twenty for tempo-controlled two-legged squats, and fifteen to twenty-five for calves. If you are doing forty repetitions of anything, the exercise is too easy rather than the rep target too low.

How often should I train legs at home?

Twice a week with at least two days between sessions. Legs are a large amount of muscle and single-leg work is demanding, so three hard leg sessions a week is more than most home trainees recover from well.

Are wall sits good for building leg strength?

They build isometric endurance and are a reasonable finisher, but they are not a substitute for loaded movement through a range. Use them at the end of a session rather than as the main exercise.
